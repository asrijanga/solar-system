## W2 · Constants and ephemeris

`pipeline/ephemeris_mars.py` writes `public/data/mars/ephemeris.json`. Run it with `npm run pipeline:ephemeris:mars`; CI re-derives the output and diffs it.

**Kernels:** the Moon's pinned `naif0012.tls`, `de440s.bsp`, `pck00011.tpc` and `gm_de440.tpc`, plus `mar099s.bsp`.
- **What `mar099s.bsp` holds:** Mars, Phobos and Deimos relative to the Mars barycentre, from 1995 to 2049 (NAIF, June 2025, per its `.cmt`).
- **Pin:** size checked against the server's 67,594,240 bytes; SHA-256 pinned at first retrieval.

**Written:**
- **Mars:**
  - Radii 3396.19 × 3396.19 × 3376.20 km (`BODY499_RADII`).
  - GM from `gm_de440.tpc`.
  - `IAU_MARS` body frame: planetocentric, east-positive.
- **Phobos and Deimos:** their radii and `IAU_PHOBOS` / `IAU_DEIMOS` frames.
- **At each canonical epoch:**
  - The Sun's and Earth's directions and distances.
  - The rotation into `IAU_MARS`.
  - Phobos's and Deimos's positions from Mars's centre, and their rotations.
  - Mars's season as its solar longitude Ls.

**Epochs.** The same two instants as the Moon's, so the whole app runs on one clock:

| Epoch | UTC | Ls | Sun distance | Mars's season |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `full-2026-01` | 2026-01-03 10:00 | 200.2° | 1.426 AU | Southern spring |
| `first-quarter-2026-01` | 2026-01-26 05:00 | 214.0° | 1.406 AU | Southern spring |

Ls 180–360° is Mars's dust-storm season. Whether the surface was clear on those dates is for W3 to establish from MARCI, not to assume.

### Checked against JPL Horizons

Fixture: `test/fixtures/horizons-mars.json`, with the requests recorded. Test: `pipeline/test_ephemeris_mars.py`.

| Check | Measured agreement | Tolerance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sub-solar point seen from Earth | 5e-5° in longitude, below 1e-6° in latitude | 0.1° (the Moon's) |
| The shipped vectors (Sun direction × rotation matrix), against the sub-solar point seen from Phobos | 8e-5° | 0.1° |
| Phobos's and Deimos's positions from Mars's centre | below 1 m. Horizons uses the same `mar099` solution, so this proves the reading, not the orbit | 1 km |

**Two conventions bit here, both recorded for the next world:**

- **Horizons gives Mars's longitudes west-positive and its latitudes planetodetic**, from its own header: "Target pole/equ : IAU_MARS {West-longitude positive}" and "Latitude is the angle between the equatorial plane and the line perpendicular to the reference ellipsoid".
  - The test converts both. For the Moon, Horizons used east longitudes.
  - A control test shows the check fails without the conversion, so it can see the difference.
- **Light time.** Earth sees Mars as it was about 20 minutes earlier, and in that time Mars turns about 5°.
  - The first version of the test compared the app's vectors with the sub-solar point seen from Earth. It failed by 4.87°.
  - For the Moon (1.3 s) the difference had been invisible.
  - The app draws Mars as it is at the instant. So those vectors are checked against Horizons seen from Phobos, where the light time is 0.03 s.
  - The sub-solar point seen from Earth is still checked against Horizons from Earth.
